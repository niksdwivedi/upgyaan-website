You have probably met them. The engineer who writes the cleanest code on the team. The analyst whose models are consistently the most accurate. The project manager who keeps the most complex programmes on track without breaking a sweat. And yet, when the promotion cycle comes around, someone else gets the nod.

This is not always unfair. Sometimes it is. But more often than people acknowledge, the differential is not competence. It is visibility.

## What visibility actually means

Visibility in a career context is not about self-promotion. It is not about posting thought-leadership content on LinkedIn every morning or speaking up loudly in meetings to establish presence. Those things can be useful, but they are often mistaken for the thing itself.

Real career visibility is simpler and harder: it means that the people who make decisions about your career — promotions, projects, references, sponsorship — have an accurate and current picture of the value you create.

Notice what that definition does not include. It does not include whether your direct manager knows your work. It does not include whether your team appreciates you. Those things matter, but they are not sufficient. The question is whether the decision-makers, the people with actual leverage over your advancement, have the information they need to advocate for you.

## The competence trap

High performers are often the most susceptible to the competence trap. The logic goes: if I do excellent work, the results will speak for themselves, and the right people will notice. This is a reasonable assumption in a small organisation where everyone has line-of-sight to everyone else's contribution. In a company of any meaningful size, it is usually wrong.

Work does not speak for itself in large organisations. It is mediated. Your results are filtered through your manager's framing, through reporting structures, through the political priorities of whoever is making the case for resources in the room you are not in.

If you are not actively shaping that framing, someone else is. And the person who is most likely shaping it in the absence of your input is your manager, who may have a different set of priorities than you do.

## The sponsorship gap

There is a difference between a mentor and a sponsor that most career advice conflates. A mentor gives you guidance. A sponsor uses their political capital to create opportunities for you — they put your name in the room, they advocate for you in conversations you are not part of, they take a reputational stake in your advancement.

Most professionals have mentors. Far fewer have sponsors. And sponsorship is almost entirely dependent on visibility. A sponsor cannot advocate for someone they do not know well, or someone whose work they do not have a clear and compelling narrative about.

Building sponsorship relationships requires being visible in the right contexts, creating opportunities for senior people to see your thinking and your judgment in action, and demonstrating that advocating for you carries low risk and clear upside for them.

## Practical visibility without the performance

Building career visibility without becoming a self-promoter requires a different mental model. Instead of broadcasting, think about creating moments of genuine value for the people whose perception matters.

**Share your thinking, not just your output.** When you solve a hard problem, write a brief note on how you approached it and what you learned. Not for applause — for the people who need to know how you think. This creates a paper trail of your judgment.

**Make your work visible upstream.** Brief your manager before big meetings so they can accurately represent your contribution. Not as a political manoeuvre, but as effective communication that serves everyone.

**Build cross-functional relationships before you need them.** The people from other teams who have seen your work, who have collaborated with you on something meaningful, are future advocates. Invest in those relationships continuously, not just when you need something.

**Create contexts for senior visibility.** Volunteer for projects that have executive sponsorship. Present findings in forums where decision-makers are present. Ask thoughtful questions in all-hands meetings that demonstrate the quality of your thinking.
