There's a moment most tech professionals recognise. You've been in your role for eighteen months. You're delivering, your manager likes you, and you've had a few "development conversations." Yet you feel no clearer about where you're going. The mentorship that was promised feels like a series of meetings where someone who benefits from your output tells you to keep doing what you're doing.

This is not an accident. It is the design.

## Corporate mentorship is optimised for retention, not growth

When a company sets up a formal mentorship programme, the unstated goal is almost always retention. Keep talented people feeling seen, feeling invested in. The mentor is almost always someone inside the organisation who is incentivised, consciously or not, to keep you engaged with the current role rather than help you find clarity on a different path.

This is not a criticism of the individuals involved. Most corporate mentors are genuinely well-intentioned. The problem is structural. When your mentor's success is measured by team output and your career growth might mean you leaving for a bigger opportunity, there is an inherent conflict of interest baked into the relationship from day one.

## The startup mentorship illusion

On the other side of the same coin sits the founder-mentor, the kind you find on advisory boards, startup networks, and accelerator programmes. These mentors are often brilliant and their stories are inspiring. But their relevance to a career in enterprise or corporate tech is frequently limited. The skills of navigating ambiguity in a twelve-person startup with no process are not the same as the skills of navigating a promotion committee in a five-thousand-person organisation.

If you are a mid-career professional in a large tech company and your mentor is a serial entrepreneur who has never had to navigate a year-end performance review calibration session, you are getting inspiration, not guidance.

## The visibility problem inside mentorship

Even well-matched mentorship often ignores one of the most important levers of career growth: visibility. You can be technically excellent, well-mentored, and completely invisible to the people who make promotion decisions. Your mentor, if they are inside your organisation, may not tell you this directly. Acknowledging that your career advancement depends heavily on political visibility rather than pure merit is uncomfortable conversation territory.

Good career mentorship has to address visibility directly. Not self-promotion in the hollow LinkedIn sense, but strategic visibility — ensuring the right people understand the value you create, in contexts where it matters.

## What useful mentorship actually looks like

Useful career mentorship shares four characteristics that most corporate programmes lack entirely.

**It is agenda-free.** The mentor has no stake in where you end up. Their measure of success is your clarity and progress, not your retention in a specific role or organisation.

**It draws on relevant lived experience.** A mentor who has navigated the specific career transitions you are approaching — from individual contributor to manager, from manager to director, from technical lead to strategic role — can offer pattern recognition that generic advice simply cannot.

**It addresses the whole picture.** Skills, visibility, relationships, positioning, timing. Career growth is a system, and a mentor who only addresses one dimension is working with an incomplete model.

**It is honest about what is hard.** Corporate careers involve politics, compromise, and decisions that are not always meritocratic. A mentor who pretends otherwise is not preparing you for the environment you are actually in.

## Building a mentorship network, not just a mentor relationship

The most effective professionals I have spoken with rarely have one mentor. They have a network of people who each offer a different lens. One person who has made the specific transition they are targeting. One person who operates outside their industry and can see their situation with fresh eyes. One person who is ahead of them by five years and can offer practical, near-horizon guidance.

Building this kind of network does not happen through formal programmes. It happens through deliberate community building, through giving before you ask, through being visible in spaces where people who are ahead of you are also present.
